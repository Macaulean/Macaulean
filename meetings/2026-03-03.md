## Summary

The team discussed challenges and potential improvements for formalizing polynomial computations and Grobner basis algorithms in Lean. They explored issues with expanding large polynomial expressions and identified bottlenecks in both Lean's kernel and the communication between Lean and Macaulay 2. The group considered simplifying the benchmark problem and proposed testing with smaller, more concrete examples, such as proving that a polynomial ideal is zero-dimensional or computing Grobner bases for simple algebraic geometry problems. They also discussed the possibility of universalizing proofs over larger fields or rings and improving the handling of ring operations in Macaulay 2. The conversation ended with plans to explore these ideas further and potentially incorporate examples from computer vision into future discussions.

## Topics

### Subalgebra Search and Gröbner Bases

Anton and Matthew discussed their efforts to find a subalgebra for the challenge benchmark, but their attempts were unsuccessful. They also explored the use of a large language model to assist in the process, though it proved unreliable. Anton expressed uncertainty about the feasibility of tackling the benchmark and expressed interest in learning more about how Gröbner bases operate. Matthew confirmed that Gröbner bases can prove their status through reductions.

### Subgrind Algorithm Functionality Overview

Anton and Matthew discussed the functionality of Subgrind, which implements an algorithm but does not explicitly seek a Gröbner basis. Damiano explained that Subgrind partitions expressions into equivalence classes and efficiently incorporates external lemmas, potentially utilizing a form of Gröbner basis, allowing it to perform broader computations.

### Polynomial Simplification Implementation Challenges

The team discussed implementing polynomial simplification and reduction algorithms, focusing on whether Grind could be used to handle large expressions. Jay suggested developing a polynomial remainder approach, while Anton proposed creating an atomic task to prove polynomial equality. The group acknowledged that while the mathematical steps are simple, implementing them in Lean is challenging due to the size of intermediate expressions and the need to control the reduction process.

### Polynomial Operations in Lean

The team discussed polynomial expression manipulation in Lean, focusing on whether existing theorems would be sufficient for their needs. Jay explained that while basic polynomial operations like addition and multiplication are supported, the system lacks deep theorems about polynomial reduction and expansion. Damiano noted that polynomials are encoded as trees rather than linear lists, which makes expansion operations computationally expensive. The group acknowledged that while polynomial expansion is possible, it would be costly for non-trivial examples, and Jay expressed interest in finding an efficient way to verify that polynomial expansions produce equivalent results.

### Optimizing Benchmark Test Calculations

The team discussed computational bottlenecks in their benchmark test, particularly focusing on the expansion of G which results in expressions with around 100,000 terms. Anton suggested using reduced syzygy bases for more efficient computations, while Jay noted that their current code could be modified to either approve identities or reduce them to unprovable forms. The discussion concluded with Jay raising a technical question about when Lean sends computations to the kernel, which Matthew clarified happens at the end of tactic execution rather than intermediate steps.

### Kernel Check and Performance Optimization

Damiano discussed a colleague named Baby who is skilled at optimizing complex computations, and suggested consulting Bhavik for help with performance issues related to permanent computations. Jay inquired about obtaining proof expressions without kernel execution, and Matthew and Damiano confirmed the existence of a "skip kernel check" flag, though they noted this could compromise soundness. The group briefly discussed the differences between elaborator and kernel checks, with Damiano mentioning that these checks cannot cover everything.

### Polynomial Degree Analysis Efficiency

Damiano and Matthew discussed an efficient approach to polynomial degree analysis, where they found that directly manipulating polynomial expressions without expansion was more efficient than traditional methods. Damiano explained that following the natural expression structure and making assumptions about the largest degree terms led to better performance. Matthew noted that Brian's code implements an S-Poly computation that adds equalities to a whiteboard for later saturation-based solving.

### Groebner Basis for Mathematical Proofs

The team discussed using Groebner basis computations for formalizing mathematical proofs, particularly focusing on polynomial equations and ideals. They explored a specific example involving the circle equation (X^2 + Y^2 = 1) and its derivatives, which Jay demonstrated could be solved using Lean and Macaulay 2. The group agreed that testing with simpler, concrete examples like Groebner basis computations for small degree ideals would be more practical than working with complex benchmark problems. They also discussed the need to improve the handling of different rings and fields in the system, with Jay noting that current limitations prevent working with anything other than rational numbers.
